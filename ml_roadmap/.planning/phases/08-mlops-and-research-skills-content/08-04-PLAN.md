**Structure (4 sub-topics, ~2-3 hours total):**

1. **The ML Publication Landscape** (~30 min): ML evolves faster than any textbook — the Transformer architecture (2017) took 3 years to become a textbook topic but was being used in industry within 18 months. Understanding the publication landscape helps you know where to look. Three tiers: (1) Preprints on arXiv (cs.LG, cs.AI, stat.ML, cs.CV, cs.CL categories) — where most ML research appears first, often months before peer review. (2) Top conferences: NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR for general ML; CVPR/ECCV for vision; ACL/EMNLP for NLP — peer-reviewed, the gold standard for credibility. (3) Industry blogs and technical reports (Google AI Blog, Meta AI, Hugging Face Blog, OpenAI technical reports) — high-quality applied research that often does not appear in academic venues. JMLR and TMLR as the main journals (slower, rigorous). Workshop papers as a source for cutting-edge early work. Teaching moment: arXiv has no peer review — treat every preprint as "interesting claim" not "established fact." Conference acceptance at NeurIPS or ICLR is the credibility signal.

2. **Curating Your Information Feed** (~45 min): The goal is a feed that surfaces important developments without requiring 4 hours of reading per day. Concrete tools and setup: arXiv Sanity Preserver (arXiv-sanity.com) for filtering arXiv by topics and popularity. Hugging Face Papers (huggingface.co/papers) curates top daily papers with discussion. Twitter/X: following specific researchers is more signal-dense than following ML accounts — name key researchers to follow (Andrej Karpathy, Ilya Sutskever, Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton, Percy Liang, Fei-Fei Li). Newsletters: The Batch (deeplearning.ai, weekly, accessible summaries), Import AI (Jack Clark, weekly, technical focus). YouTube for visual learners: Yannic Kilcher (deep paper walkthroughs), Two Minute Papers (accessible summaries). Reddit r/MachineLearning for community discussion and paper announcements. Papers With Code for practical implementation context alongside research. Concrete setup: pick ONE arXiv feed, ONE newsletter, and ONE social channel — do not try to follow all simultaneously.

3. **Deep Dives vs Broad Awareness** (~30 min): The 80/20 approach: 80% broad awareness (headline + abstract), 20% deep engagement (full paper or second-pass). Broad awareness means you know what has been published and can search for it when you need it. Deep engagement means you understand enough to apply or build on it. "Just-in-time" learning: go deep on a paper when you are about to implement the technique, not proactively. Information overload is not a knowledge problem — it is a filtering problem. The goal is not to read every important paper; it is to know which papers to read when you need them. Topics-focused following vs general awareness: if you work primarily in NLP, follow ACL/EMNLP proceedings and NLP researchers — not all of ML. Avoid the anxiety of "falling behind" — no one reads everything; the practitioners who are most current have good filters, not more hours.

4. **Community Engagement** (~30 min): Reading groups (local or online) provide accountability and discussion. The NeurIPS virtual attendance option makes top conferences accessible without travel cost. Contributing to open-source (Hugging Face Transformers, PyTorch, scikit-learn) puts you in direct contact with researchers. Engaging on social media: sharing paper summaries or implementation notes builds your professional network and sharpens your own understanding. ML Discord servers and Slack communities (e.g., Hugging Face Discord, fast.ai forums) for real-time discussion. Conferences as networking: NeurIPS/ICML have workshops, poster sessions, and social events — more valuable for meeting people than attending talks (talks are available online afterward).

**Prerequisite admonition:** Complete the Reading Papers page before this one — you need to know how to read a paper before knowing which papers to follow.
**Time/Difficulty:** ~2-3 hours | Beginner-Intermediate
**Learning outcomes (4 bullets):** understand the ML publication landscape, set up a sustainable information feed, distinguish when to do a deep dive vs broad awareness scan, find community resources for accountability and networking.

**Why Research Skills? (2-3 paragraphs):**
ML evolves faster than any other engineering discipline — the gap between "what textbooks teach" and "what practitioners use" is measured in months, not years. The Transformer architecture appeared in 2017 and was textbook material by 2020, but practitioners were building production NLP systems on it by 2018. If you only learn from textbooks and courses, you will always be 2-3 years behind the state of the art.

Research literacy is not just for researchers — it is the skill that separates ML engineers who can implement techniques from those who wait for them to appear in Keras or scikit-learn. Reading the original BERT paper, reproducing the training loop, understanding the design choices — this is what makes architectural decisions legible rather than magical.

The research skills section teaches three complementary processes: how to read papers efficiently and critically, how to implement them to deepen understanding, and how to stay current without being overwhelmed. Together, these complete the transition from "understands ML" to "can grow with ML."

**Why This Path admonition:** Keshav's three-pass method (the canonical academic reference), Andrew Ng's practical advice (reading a paper a day for two months to enter a new field), and the ML Reproducibility Challenge (NeurIPS community-endorsed reproduction practice) all converge on the same core skills taught here. Most ML courses stop at model evaluation — this section picks up where they leave off.
